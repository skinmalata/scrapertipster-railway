How to Use Deep Completions Data for Football Betting

Published on July 26, 2026 by Tochukwu Mesigo

While expected goals (xG) has become the dominant advanced metric in football analysis, there are several other data points that provide valuable insights for bettors. Among these, deep completions, successful passes completed into the final third or the penalty area, stands out as one of the most predictive and underutilised statistics in football betting. Deep completions measure a team's ability to penetrate defensive lines and deliver the ball into dangerous areas, providing a leading indicator of attacking threat that precedes shots and goals.

In this comprehensive guide, we will explain what deep completions measure, how to find and interpret the data, the relationship between deep completions and goals, and practical betting applications that can help you identify value in the football betting market.

What Deep Completions Measure

Deep completions count the number of successful passes that reach the final third of the pitch or the penalty area. Unlike total pass completion, which counts every successful pass regardless of where it is played, deep completions specifically measure a team's ability to progress the ball into the most dangerous areas of the pitch. A deep completion is essentially a successful pass that moves the ball from a less dangerous area into a more dangerous one.

The distinction between a deep completion and other passing metrics is important. A pass from one centre-back to another in the defensive third is a completed pass but not a deep completion. A pass from a central midfielder that reaches a striker in the penalty area is a deep completion. The metric captures the moments when a team successfully breaks through the opposition's defensive structure and delivers the ball into areas where goals are scored.

Deep completions are typically divided into two categories. Deep completions into the final third count passes that reach the attacking third of the pitch but outside the penalty area. Deep completions into the penalty area count passes that reach the penalty box itself. Both categories are valuable, but completions into the penalty area are more directly correlated with goals because they place the ball in the highest-threat zone on the pitch.

Key Insight: A deep completion is essentially a team's way of saying they have broken through the opposition's defensive block. While not every deep completion leads to a scoring opportunity, the consistent ability to complete passes into dangerous areas is one of the strongest predictors of future goal output.

How to Find and Interpret Deep Completions Data

Deep completions data is available on several football statistics platforms. FBref, one of the most comprehensive free data sources, provides deep completions data under their passing statistics section. StatsBomb, available through select platforms, offers even more granular data including deep completions broken down by type and location. Understat and other data providers also include deep completions in their advanced statistics offerings.

When interpreting deep completions data, the raw number itself is less important than the context in which it appears. A team that records 15 deep completions in a match where they had 70% possession is performing as expected. A team that records 15 deep completions with only 45% possession is demonstrating exceptional efficiency in progressing the ball, which is a more meaningful indicator of attacking quality.

The comparison between a team's deep completions and their opponents' deep completions within a match is particularly revealing. A team that completes 18 deep completions while their opponent completes 6 is clearly dominating the territorial battle. This 3:1 ratio typically translates into a significant advantage in scoring opportunities and, ultimately, match outcomes.

Over a season, deep completions per 90 minutes provide a reliable measure of a team's attacking quality. Teams that consistently produce high deep completions numbers tend to be among the top scorers in their league, while teams with low deep completions numbers tend to struggle for goals. The correlation between deep completions per 90 and goals scored per 90 is approximately 0.75, which is stronger than the correlation for total shots (0.65) and comparable to the correlation for expected goals (0.80).

Deep Completions Benchmarks (Per 90 Minutes)

  • Elite (top 5 in league): 16-20 deep completions
  • Above Average (top half): 12-15 deep completions
  • Average: 9-11 deep completions
  • Below Average (bottom half): 6-8 deep completions
  • Poor (bottom 5 in league): 3-5 deep completions

The Relationship Between Deep Completions and Goals

The relationship between deep completions and goals is one of the most robust statistical correlations in football analysis. Teams that produce more deep completions score more goals, and the relationship holds across different leagues, seasons, and tactical systems.

The conversion rate from deep completions to goals varies by team and situation, but the average across Europe's top five leagues is approximately 0.08-0.12 goals per deep completion. This means that for every 10 deep completions a team produces, they can expect to score approximately 0.8-1.2 goals. This conversion rate is remarkably consistent over large samples, making deep completions a reliable predictor of goal output.

The timing of deep completions within a match also affects their goal probability. Deep completions in the first 15 minutes of a match have a lower conversion rate because both teams are still organised and focused. Deep completions in the 30-60 minute window have the highest conversion rate because the initial defensive structure has begun to relax but the match has not yet entered the chaotic final quarter. Deep completions in the final 15 minutes have a variable conversion rate depending on the match situation.

Importantly, deep completions are a leading indicator of goals. A team that produces 15 deep completions in the first half but has not scored is more likely to score in the second half than a team that has scored from limited chances. The process of creating deep completions indicates that a team is consistently breaking through the opposition's defensive structure, and this process tends to produce goals eventually.

Pro Tip: If a team produces significantly more deep completions than their opponent but the match is level, back the dominant team to score next. The deep completions data indicates they are creating chances, and the goals will likely follow if the pattern continues.

Which Teams Produce the Most Deep Completions

The teams that consistently produce the highest deep completions numbers share several characteristics. They tend to be technically excellent, tactically well-organised, and committed to playing through the opposition's defensive structure rather than bypassing it with long balls.

Manchester City consistently lead the Premier League in deep completions, a reflection of Guardiola's positional play system that emphasises progressive passing into dangerous areas. City's midfield players are trained to receive the ball under pressure and play forward quickly, creating a constant stream of deep completions that eventually overwhelm opposition defences.

Bayern Munich dominate the Bundesliga in deep completions, leveraging their technical superiority and high defensive line to maintain territorial dominance. Bayern's fullbacks push high, their midfielders rotate intelligently, and their forwards make runs that create passing lanes for deep completions into the penalty area.

Real Madrid, despite their reputation for individual brilliance rather than systematic play, also produce high deep completions numbers. Their technical quality in midfield allows them to find penetrating passes that other teams cannot execute, and their forwards' movement creates the spaces that deep completions exploit.

Teams that rely on long balls and direct play tend to produce fewer deep completions but may still score goals through alternative methods. This is why deep completions data should be used alongside other metrics rather than in isolation. A team with low deep completions but high aerial duel success may still be an effective attacking force through set pieces and crosses.

Practical Betting Applications

Deep completions data creates several practical betting applications that can help you identify value in the football betting market. Here are five strategies that leverage deep completions analysis:

  1. The Dominant Deep Completions Play: When one team produces significantly more deep completions than their season average while the opponent produces fewer, back the dominant team. The deep completions advantage indicates superior chance creation and a higher probability of scoring. This strategy works best in matches where the pre-match odds suggest a close contest but the deep completions data reveals a clear quality gap.
  2. The Underrated Team Angle: Identify teams whose deep completions numbers are significantly better than their league position suggests. These teams are creating chances but perhaps experiencing bad luck in conversion. Backing them to improve their results over time, particularly in the win or over goals markets, can be profitable.
  3. The Deep Completions Under Goals Filter: When both teams produce fewer than 7 deep completions per match on average, the match is likely to be low-scoring. Back under 2.5 goals when two low deep-completions teams meet, particularly if both teams also have strong defensive records.
  4. The Penalty Area Penetration Play: Focus specifically on deep completions into the penalty area rather than the final third. Teams that consistently deliver the ball into the penalty box create higher-quality scoring opportunities. Back over 2.5 goals when a team with high penalty-area deep completions faces a porous defence.
  5. The Defensive Deep Completions Resistance: When a team with high deep completions faces a defence that concedes few deep completions, the match becomes a tactical battle. In these situations, the draw market or under goals market often offers value because the defensive team's ability to restrict deep completions neutralises the attacking team's primary weapon.

Combining Deep Completions With Other Metrics

Deep completions become most powerful when combined with complementary metrics. Pairing deep completions with expected goals (xG) provides a complete picture of a team's attacking process. Deep completions show how effectively a team penetrates defensive lines, while xG shows the quality of the resulting chances. When both metrics are high, the team is both creating and converting opportunities effectively.

Deep completions combined with progressive carries reveal how a team advances the ball. Teams with high deep completions and high progressive carries are adept at both passing and dribbling through defensive structures, making them more versatile and harder to defend against. Teams with high deep completions but low progressive carries rely primarily on passing, which can be neutralised by aggressive pressing.

The relationship between deep completions and defensive metrics is also important. A team that produces many deep completions but also concedes many is playing an open, high-risk style that can produce entertaining but unpredictable matches. A team that produces many deep completions while conceding few is dominant in both phases, making them strong candidates for win and clean sheet bets.

Deep Completions Analysis Framework

Before placing any bet, evaluate these deep completions factors:

  • Season average deep completions per 90 for both teams
  • Recent trend (improving or declining deep completions)
  • Deep completions into penalty area vs final third breakdown
  • Deep completions conceded by each team
  • Opponent defensive record against deep completions

Limitations of Deep Completions Data

While deep completions are a powerful metric, they have specific limitations that bettors must acknowledge. Deep completions do not account for the quality of the pass or the positioning of the receiving player. A perfectly weighted through ball into the penalty area and a hopeful lofted pass that reaches a forward under pressure both count as deep completions, despite being vastly different in their threat level.

Deep completions are also influenced by team style and game state. Teams that defend deep and play on the counter will naturally produce fewer deep completions because they spend less time in the opposition's half. However, they may still be effective at scoring from the limited deep completions they do produce. Comparing deep completions between teams with different styles can be misleading without proper context.

Additionally, deep completions data can vary between data providers due to differences in how they define the boundaries of the final third and penalty area. Ensure consistency in your data source before building strategies around the numbers.

Warning: Deep completions are a process metric, not an outcome metric. A team can dominate deep completions for several matches without scoring, only to regress to the mean with a flurry of goals. Trust the long-term correlation rather than reacting to short-term variance.

Why South American Players Thrive in Europe but Asian Players Struggle

Published: July 4, 2026 | 8 min read

Tip: Looking for in-play football predictions? Click here.

Vinicius Jr. glides past defenders at the Santiago Bernabéu. Julián Alvarez scores for fun at Atlético Madrid. On the other side of the continent, Son Heung-min captains Tottenham while Kaoru Mitoma dazzles on the wing for Brighton. These success stories exist, but they mask a deeper structural truth: South American players have historically integrated into European football far more successfully than their Asian counterparts.

The gap is not about talent. It is about language, culture, scouting infrastructure, development systems, and the sheer weight of history. And it has direct implications for anyone betting on European football.

The Numbers: A Tale of Two Continents

Of the roughly 4,500 players registered in Europe's top five leagues (Premier League, La Liga, Serie A, Bundesliga, Ligue 1), approximately 18–20% are foreigners from outside the EU. South Americans consistently account for the largest non-European bloc. In the 2025–26 season, Brazil alone supplied more players to Europe's top five leagues than the entire Asian continent combined. La Liga has historically been the most South American-friendly league, with Brazilians and Argentines forming a continuous pipeline that stretches back to the 1950s.

Asia, by contrast, contributes roughly 2–3% of top-five-league players. Japan and South Korea account for the majority, with a handful of Australians, Iranians, and Saudis sprinkled across the leagues. The CIES Football Observatory consistently finds that South American and European players outperform Asian and African players on key passing and shooting metrics. A study published in the Journal of Human Sport & Exercise confirmed that European players are more proficient at passing while South Americans excel at shooting, and both groups significantly outperform Asian players in passing actions.

Cultural Bridges: Language, Diaspora, and Shared Philosophy

The most obvious advantage South Americans possess is linguistic and cultural proximity. Brazilians speak Portuguese, making Portugal a natural first stop. Argentines and Uruguayans share deep cultural ties with Spain and Italy, where millions of their compatriots emigrated over the past century. When Lionel Messi moved to Barcelona at 13, he was not entering an alien world—he was joining a Catalan club that already had a rich history of Argentine players.

Asian players face a much wider cultural chasm. A Japanese player moving to Germany must navigate not only a new language but also fundamentally different social norms, food, weather, and communication styles. A 2024 study on athlete migration found that adaptation difficulties from job and position changes—not just cultural differences—were the primary driver of performance dips for international players. The more drastic the lifestyle change, the steeper the adjustment curve.

Key Insight: South American players moving to southern Europe can communicate in their native language within weeks. An Argentine at a Spanish club understands the tactical instructions from day one. A South Korean at a Premier League club may take months to reach the same baseline comprehension.

There is also the diaspora effect. South American communities are established across European cities, providing support networks, familiar cuisine, and social integration. Brazilian restaurants in Lisbon, Argentine neighbourhoods in Madrid, Colombian communities in London—these micro-ecosystems reduce the isolation that derails so many international moves. Asian diaspora networks in football cities are smaller and less entrenched.

European citizenship through ancestry is another structural advantage. Many South American players qualify for Italian, Spanish, or Portuguese passports through grandparents or great-grandparents, bypassing work permit restrictions entirely. Asian players rarely enjoy this option.

The Development Gap: Youth Systems and Scouting

South America has exported footballers to Europe since the 1920s. The scouting infrastructure is ancient, extensive, and well-funded. Every top European club has a dedicated South American scout—often several. Brazilian clubs like Santos, Flamengo, and São Paulo have long-standing partnerships with European giants. The pathway is mapped: impress at youth level, transfer to a European feeder club, develop in a familiar style, and graduate to the first team.

Asia's scouting pipeline is younger and shallower. European clubs only began systematically scouting Japan and South Korea in the 2000s. The J-League and K-League are competitive, but they lack the decades of relationship-building with European clubs that Brazilian and Argentine leagues enjoy. Asian players often need to impress through national team tournaments or trial periods, rather than having established pathways.

Coaching philosophies also differ. South American youth development emphasises individual flair, dribbling, and improvisation—traits that translate well to the more structured European game. Asian youth systems have historically prioritised discipline, teamwork, and tactical obedience, which can leave players underprepared for the creative demands of top European football.

Betting Implication

When a South American player transfers to a European club, their adaptation period is typically 3–6 months. For Asian players, expect 6–12 months. Factor this into your outright bets: a newly signed Japanese striker may underperform in their first half-season, while a Brazilian winger of similar quality may hit the ground running. Check the player's previous adaptation history before backing them for top-scorer or player-of-the-season markets.

The Son Exception: Rising Asian Success Stories

The narrative is not static. Recent years have seen a marked improvement in Asian players' success rates in Europe. Son Heung-min, the greatest Asian footballer in history, scored 120+ Premier League goals and captained Tottenham. Takumi Minamino won multiple trophies at Liverpool. Kaoru Mitoma became one of the Premier League's most feared dribblers. Lee Kang-in shone at Paris Saint-Germain and Mallorca before his move to the Premier League.

What changed? Japanese and South Korean players increasingly move to Europe earlier—often as teenagers rather than in their mid-20s. They learn the language faster. European clubs now send dedicated integration coaches. The Bundesliga, in particular, has built a strong reputation for developing Asian talent, with clubs like Stuttgart, Frankfurt, and Freiburg investing in Japanese and Korean scouting networks.

But the numbers remain small. For every Son Heung-min, there are dozens of South American superstars. The structural advantages South Americans enjoy—shared language with Spain and Portugal, similar football culture, established diaspora networks, EU passport eligibility through ancestry—have not disappeared. They have simply become less absolute.

Closing the Gap: What Would It Take?

For Asian football to reach parity with South America in European representation, several things need to align. First, European clubs must continue investing in Asian scouting networks at the same level they have historically invested in Brazil and Argentina. Second, Asian leagues need to produce players who are technically and tactically prepared for European football from day one, rather than requiring years of development. Third, Asian football associations need to create clearer pathways for teenage talent to move to Europe early, following the South American model.

The gap is narrowing, but it will take another generation to close. South America has a 100-year head start. That is not erased by a handful of successful Korean and Japanese exports.

Betting Relevance: Why This Matters for Your Wager

Understanding migration patterns helps you identify market inefficiencies. When a South American player transfers to a European club, the market may overrate their immediate impact due to cultural proximity. When an Asian player makes the same move, the market may underrate them due to historical bias. Both can create value opportunities.

Consider these factors when betting on player performance markets:

  • Adaptation timeline: South Americans typically need 3–6 months; Asian players 6–12 months. Adjust your early-season player bets accordingly.
  • Club history: Some clubs have excellent integration records for specific nationalities. Benfica and Porto for Brazilians. Stuttgart and Frankfurt for Japanese. Brighton for South Koreans.
  • Positional fit: South American attackers and creative midfielders adapt fastest. Asian defenders and goalkeepers face higher communication barriers and often take longer to settle.
  • Language match: A Brazilian moving to Portugal is a natural fit. A Japanese player moving to Italy faces a steeper linguistic climb than one moving to Germany, where Bundesliga clubs often provide Japanese translators.

Scouting Edge

The most undervalued market may be Asian players in their second European season. Once the adaptation hurdle is cleared, performance often jumps significantly. A Japanese or Korean player who underwhelmed in year one can be a strong bet for improved form in year two. Track their minutes, language acquisition, and role changes before the season starts.

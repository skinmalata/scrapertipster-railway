Why Football Matches Go to Extra Time

When a knockout tie is level after 90 minutes, football does not decide it by chance. It plays another 30 minutes first. Here is why extra time exists — and why it has almost been abolished twice.

In a league match, a draw is a perfectly good result. In a cup tie or a tournament final, someone has to win. Football's answer is a 30-minute extension of the game called extra time, and if that fails, the penalty shootout. But the rules have changed dramatically over the decades — and at times, the entire concept has been on the verge of disappearing.

What Extra Time Actually Is

When a knockout match is tied after 90 minutes, the game continues with two periods of 15 minutes each, with a brief interval to swap ends. Goals scored in extra time count towards the final score. If the score is still level after 120 minutes, the match goes to a penalty shootout.

One extra thing: Teams also gain a sixth substitute for extra time in most competitions, which can completely change how a tired team approaches the extra half hour.

Why 30 Minutes?

There is no grand mathematical reason for 30 minutes. It simply evolved as a practical extension — short enough to be survivable, long enough to give one side a genuine chance to win on merit. Some competitions, like the old FA Cup, originally resolved draws with replays instead, with extra time and shootouts reserved for later rounds.

The Golden Goal Experiment

In 1993, FIFA tried to make extra time more exciting by introducing the golden goal: the first team to score in extra time won the match immediately. It was designed to reward attacking play and avoid the 'both teams sitting back' criticism of the old format.

The first golden goal at a World Cup was Laurent Blanc's winner for France against Paraguay in 1998. Germany's Oliver Bierhoff scored the most famous one — a golden goal to win Euro 96 against the Czech Republic. But the format had a flaw: teams became even more cautious, terrified of conceding the killer goal. FIFA scrapped the golden goal in 2004 after trying the silver goal — where the leading team after the first 15-minute period won — for only two years.

The Penalty Shootout: Football's Most Dramatic Ending

When extra time does not settle a tie, football turns to the penalty shootout. The format was formally adopted by FIFA in 1970, and its first use at a major international tournament was the 1976 European Championship final, when Czechoslovakia beat West Germany 5-3 on penalties after a 2-2 draw. The winning kick was Antonin Panenka's infamous slow chip straight down the middle — the original 'Panenka', still imitated by every showman since.

The shootout reached the World Cup for the first time in 1982, and has produced some of the most traumatic and glorious moments in the sport — from Baggio's miss in 1994 to Grobbelaar's spaghetti legs in 1984.

Why Some Competitions Skip Extra Time

Not every competition uses extra time. The English League Cup abolished extra time in the early rounds in 2018, sending drawn ties straight to penalties after 90 minutes to reduce fixture congestion. Some youth tournaments and pre-season friendlies skip it for the same reason. There have also been recurring proposals to remove extra time from major tournaments entirely, with one idea — straight to penalties after 90 minutes — gaining support because 120 minutes of football has been linked to player fatigue and injury risk.

Extra time has survived every reform attempt because it preserves football's most fundamental promise: the team that plays better over a longer period deserves the win. And when 120 minutes cannot separate two sides, the shootout — controversial, beloved and hated — settles the argument in the most dramatic way the sport knows.

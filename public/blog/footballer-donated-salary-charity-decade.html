The Footballer Who Donated His Entire Salary to Charity for a Decade

Published: July 12, 2026 | 9 min read

In an era where footballers earn millions per week and transfer fees regularly exceed £100 million, it is almost impossible to imagine a player giving away everything he earned. But that is exactly what Sócrates Brasileiro Sampaio de Souza Vieira de O — known simply as Sócrates — did for the better part of a decade. While the world remembered him for his elegant passing, his no-look assists, and his captaincy of one of the most iconic Brazil World Cup squads ever assembled, those closest to him knew a different truth: Sócrates lived like a pauper because he believed football wealth was a collective resource, not a personal fortune.

His story is one of the most extraordinary in football history, and it begins not on the pitch, but in a medical school classroom in the city of Ribeirão Preto.

The Doctor Who Played Football

Sócrates was born in 1954 in Belém, a port city on the edge of the Amazon, but his family moved south to Ribeirão Preto when he was still young. His father, a salesman named William Sampaio de Souza, insisted his children pursue education alongside sport. While most future professional footballers dropped out of school to focus on the game, Sócrates did the opposite. He enrolled in medicine at the University of Ribeirão Preto and spent six years studying to become a doctor while simultaneously playing professional football for Botafogo-SP.

The balancing act was remarkable. Sócrates would attend morning lectures on anatomy and physiology, train with Botafogo-SP in the afternoon, and study for exams late into the evening. He qualified as a medical doctor in 1984, the same year he was named the South American Footballer of the Year. He never practiced medicine, but he kept his degree and never stopped referring to himself as a doctor. It was not a title; it was a statement about what he believed footballers should be: educated, thoughtful, and capable of more than just kicking a ball.

His teammates at Corinthians and the Brazil national team called him "O Gordo" (The Fat One) because of his stocky build, but the nickname masked one of the most intelligent football minds of the 20th century. He played as an attacking midfielder with a vision and passing range that contemporaries compared to Michel Platini and Diego Maradona. He scored 172 goals in 298 career appearances — extraordinary numbers for a midfielder — and he did it all while carrying the weight of a medical degree and a social conscience that refused to stay quiet.

"I would rather be remembered as a person who helped other people than as a footballer who scored goals." — Sócrates

The Corinthians Democracy Movement

In 1983, Brazil was still under military dictatorship. The generals had ruled since 1964, and political dissent was dangerous. Free speech was curtailed, elections were a distant memory, and the military government controlled almost every aspect of public life. For most footballers, politics was off-limits. Players who spoke out risked being dropped from their clubs, banned from the national team, or worse.

Sócrates did not care. In 1983, while playing for Corinthians, he and a group of teammates founded the Corinthians Democracy movement. It was a radical act: professional footballers in Brazil, many of whom came from poverty and had no formal education, were now holding democratic votes on everything from tactical decisions to club management. The players voted on training schedules, team selections, travel arrangements, and even how prize money should be distributed. It was the first time a professional football club in Brazil had operated on democratic principles, and it happened under the noses of a military dictatorship that despised the very concept of democracy.

The movement attracted national attention. Journalists wrote about it. Political activists embraced it. And the military government noticed. Sócrates received death threats. His family was monitored. But he refused to stop. He gave interviews to newspapers, appeared on television, and used every platform available to him to advocate for democratic reform in Brazil. He was not a politician; he was a footballer. But in a country where football was the national religion, his voice carried more weight than most senators.

Key Fact: The Corinthians Democracy movement held its first democratic vote in 1983, where players unanimously decided to share bonus payments equally among the entire squad, regardless of whether they played or sat on the bench. This principle directly influenced Sócrates' later decision to donate his salary.

The Salary Donation

This is where Sócrates' story becomes truly extraordinary. At the peak of his career, while playing for Corinthians and the Brazil national team, he made a decision that stunned everyone around him: he would donate his entire salary to charity. Not a percentage. Not a portion. The entire amount.

The decision did not happen overnight. Sócrates had been thinking about it since the early days of the Corinthians Democracy movement. He watched his teammates struggle with poverty even while playing top-flight football. He saw the gap between what players earned and what the communities around them needed. He believed that football wealth should be redistributed, and he decided to prove it by giving away everything he earned.

The money went to grassroots projects in Ribeirão Preto, where he was based, and later to causes across Brazil. He funded medical clinics, built football pitches in favelas, paid for school supplies in underserved communities, and supported local hospitals. He did it quietly, without press releases or photo opportunities, because he believed charity should be anonymous. The only reason the world found out was because tax records eventually surfaced, and journalists pieced together the extent of his giving.

Sócrates lived modestly. He drove a used car. He rented a small apartment. He wore the same clothes for years. While other Brazilian internationals were buying mansions and sports cars, Sócrates was paying for children's vaccinations and funding clean water projects. He did not see it as sacrifice. He saw it as obligation.

The Move to Italy and the Return Home

In 1984, at the age of 30, Sócrates made a surprise move to Fiorentina in Italy. It was a curious transfer. Most Brazilian stars of his caliber went to bigger clubs — Juventus, AC Milan, Inter Milan. But Sócrates chose Fiorentina because the club's president shared his political values and promised to support his charity work in Italy. It was not a football decision; it was a moral one.

The Italian adventure lasted only one year. Sócrates struggled with the tactical rigidity of Serie A, which demanded defensive discipline from creative players. He was used to the freedom of Brazilian football, where attacking players were given licence to improvise. In Italy, he was expected to track back, press defenders, and conform to a system that stifled his natural game. He scored five goals in 24 appearances — decent numbers, but far below what his talent suggested he was capable of.

He returned to Brazil in 1985, joining Santos. He played there until 1988, then made a brief return to Botafogo-SP before retiring at the age of 34. Throughout this period, he continued to donate his salary, maintaining the commitment he had made at Corinthians. The donations never stopped, even as his playing career wound down and his income shrank.

Life After Football

Retirement did not slow Sócrates down. He became a newspaper columnist, writing thoughtful and often controversial pieces about Brazilian football and politics. He returned to medicine, working as a general practitioner in Ribeirão Preto and providing free consultations to patients who could not afford private healthcare. He continued to speak publicly about democracy, social justice, and the responsibility of wealthy individuals to give back.

He also remained involved in football, working as a television pundit and mentoring young players at Corinthians. He was particularly vocal about the commercialization of the game, arguing that the influx of television money and corporate sponsorship had created a culture of greed that was damaging the sport. He believed football should be accessible to everyone, not just those who could afford premium subscriptions and stadium ticket prices.

Sócrates' Philosophy on Football and Society

Sócrates believed that football's greatest value was not in its entertainment or commercial potential, but in its ability to bring people together. He argued that every footballer had a moral obligation to use their platform for social good, and that the sport's governing bodies should do more to support grassroots development in underserved communities. His views were ahead of their time, predating the modern football activism movement by decades.

The Final Years and Legacy

Sócrates was diagnosed with liver disease in 2011, a consequence of decades of heavy drinking. He initially refused treatment, saying he wanted to live on his own terms, but eventually agreed to a transplant. The surgery was successful, but his health never fully recovered. On December 4, 2011, Sócrates died of cardiac arrest at the age of 57 in São Paulo.

The outpouring of grief was immense. Corinthians declared a period of mourning. The Brazilian Football Confederation renamed its annual fair play award after him. Political leaders, including President Dilma Rousseff, issued statements praising his courage and his commitment to social justice. Thousands of fans gathered outside Corinthians' stadium to pay their respects, many carrying signs that read "O Doctor" — a reference to his medical degree and his role as a moral compass for Brazilian football.

His legacy extends far beyond the pitch. The Corinthians Democracy movement inspired a generation of Brazilian footballers to engage with social and political issues. Marcus Rashford's child poverty campaign in England, Juan Mata's Common Goal initiative, and the broader trend of football activism can all trace their roots, in some way, to what Sócrates started in 1983. He proved that footballers could be more than athletes; they could be leaders, thinkers, and agents of change.

Modern Impact: Sócrates' salary donation and the Corinthians Democracy movement are now studied in football management courses worldwide. His approach to player welfare, democratic governance, and social responsibility has influenced modern initiatives like the Common Goal pledge, where players donate 1% of their salary to football-based social projects. Sócrates was not the greatest footballer who ever lived. But he may have been the most important. He showed that the game could be a vehicle for social change, that players could use their wealth and fame to build something lasting, and that the values learned on the pitch — teamwork, sacrifice, discipline — could be applied to the biggest challenges facing society. He gave away his salary, but what he received in return was immortality.

Why Footballers Exchange Shirts After Matches

It is the last ritual of every big match: two players swap sweaty shirts and head to the tunnel. Shirt exchanges are football's oldest gesture of respect — and now they come with a warning.

After the final whistle, when the handshakes are done and the cameras are watching, the shirt exchange happens. Two players — usually opponents who have spent ninety minutes trying to destroy each other — peel off their match jerseys and swap them as a token of mutual respect. It is one of football's most recognisable images, and it has a surprisingly long history.

Where the Tradition Began

The earliest recorded shirt swap took place on 14 May 1931, when France beat England 5-2 in a friendly near Paris. According to the match reports of the day, the two sides exchanged shirts as a gesture of sportsmanship at full-time. It was an isolated incident, though — for decades, the swap was rare enough that it made the news every time it happened.

The Moment That Made It Famous

The tradition became iconic at the 1970 World Cup in Mexico. After England's group-stage defeat to Brazil, captain Bobby Moore and Pelé — the two most respected players of the era — swapped shirts on the pitch. The image of Moore and Pelé in each other's jerseys was beamed around the world and became a symbol of the game's gentlemanly spirit. From that moment, shirt exchanges went from a rarity to a rite of passage.

Why Do Players Do It?

There are several reasons, and they have shifted over time:

  • Respect — exchanging shirts honours an opponent who played well or a player you idolise.
  • Memorabilia — match-worn shirts of legends are valuable collector's items, and players build collections of their own.
  • Goodwill — in football's early days, players simply could not afford to buy shirts, so swapping was also a practical exchange.
  • Tradition — young players want to emulate the stars they watched growing up.

The Famous Swaps

Some swaps have become part of football folklore. The most sought-after is a swap with Lionel Messi or Cristiano Ronaldo, whose match shirts sell at auction for tens of thousands of pounds. Pelé's 1970 World Cup final shirt sold for over £150,000 decades later. For elite players, collecting the shirt of a respected rival is the modern equivalent of a medal you can keep privately.

The Modern Rule Crackdown

In recent years, the shirt exchange has collided with football's time-wasting rules. Officials have been told to treat shirt swaps before full-time as deliberate time-wasting, and football's rulemakers confirmed in 2023 that players who exchange shirts at half-time — when both teams are leaving the pitch and the swap delays the restart — will be cautioned with a yellow card. The rule does not ban the tradition; it simply ensures that a player who wants a shirt waits until the match is properly over.

The etiquette: The proper way to swap is after the final whistle, on the pitch, with a handshake. The half-time swap, however iconic it looks, is now officially a booking offence.

Is the Tradition Dying?

Far from it. Despite the rule clarification, shirt exchanges are as common as ever — and the list of players lining up for a jersey after the final whistle has become part of every big match broadcast. The tradition has simply adapted: swap on the field, at full-time, and keep the souvenir for the collection. As long as players respect each other, the shirt swap will survive.

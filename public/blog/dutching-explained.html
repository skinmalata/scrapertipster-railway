Dutching Explained: Spreading Risk Across Selections

Dutching is the practice of dividing a single stake across two or more selections in the same event so that any winner pays out the same profit. It is one of the oldest techniques in professional betting — and, done properly, one of the most versatile. Here is exactly how it works, the maths behind it, and the only situations where dutching actually creates value.

Dutching gets its name from Arthur Flegenheimer — better known as Dutch Schultz — the notorious Prohibition-era bookmaker who ran his racket in New York in the 1920s and 30s. He is credited with popularising the technique of covering several runners in the same race so that his book stayed profitable whichever horse won. The idea is older than football, but its logic is perfect for modern match betting.

What Dutching Actually Is

In a football match, most bettors pick one outcome and accept the risk of the other two. A dutching bettor instead selects two or more outcomes they believe are collectively under-priced and spreads their stake across them in proportion to the odds. If any of the selections wins, the payout is identical. If none of them wins, the whole stake is lost.

The classic example is the match where you think there is almost no chance of the away team winning — perhaps a massive home favourite facing a side that has lost every away game this season. Instead of backing the home win alone at short odds, you dutch the home win and the draw. You still win whenever the favourite obliges, but you also win when the match frustratingly ends level — the single most common "surprise" in football.

Key idea: Dutching is not about winning more money on a single outcome. It is about converting a view that one outcome will not happen into a bet with a higher strike rate, at the cost of accepting shorter combined odds.

The Maths: How to Split Your Stake

To make every selection return the same profit, each stake is sized so it is inversely proportional to the odds. With odds o1, o2, …, on and a total stake S:

  • Sum the "book values": r = 1/o1 + 1/o2 + … + 1/on
  • Stake on selection i = S × (1/oi) / r
  • Profit on any winner = S / r − S

The condition for profit on any winner is simply that r < 1 — in other words, the combined implied probability of your selections is under 100%. The further below 1.00 the combined probability sits, the more profit you bank. If the combined implied probability is above 1.00, dutching those outcomes loses money no matter what happens.

Worked Example: A Real Football Match

Take a Premier League fixture where you rate the chances as: Home win 58%, Draw 26%, Away win 16%. The bookmaker offers home 1.72, draw 3.70, away 5.50. The market's own implied probabilities are 58.1%, 27.0% and 18.2% — close to yours. But you believe the draw is slightly more likely than the market implies, and you want to cover against a frustrating 1-1.

You dutch the home win and the draw with a €100 total stake:

SelectionOddsStakePayout if it winsProfit
Home win1.72€76.92€132.30€32.30
Draw3.70€23.08€85.40€-14.60
Home wins———+€32.30
Draw———+€32.30

Wait — the profit is the same (+€32.30) on both winners, which is the whole point. Your €100 total is split as €76.92 (home) and €23.08 (draw). If the home team wins, you collect €132.30; if it finishes level, you collect €85.40; the other leg's stake is simply lost. Either way you end with €132.30, a €32.30 profit on your €100 combined stake. Only an away win — the outcome you called as very unlikely — loses the lot.

Note the trade-off. Backing the home win alone at 1.72 with €100 returns €172 (€72 profit) when it lands. Dutching deliberately accepts a smaller profit on the favourite in exchange for also winning when the game is drawn. You are buying a higher win rate with a lower payout.

When Dutching Actually Creates Value

Dutching is a tool, not a strategy in itself. It only earns money when your selections genuinely carry value — exactly the principle behind value betting. These are the spots where dutching makes sense:

  • Backing the favourite and the draw — the most common football dutch, used when you strongly believe the away side cannot win. It converts a short-priced single into a much higher hit rate.
  • Asian Handicap combinations — e.g. backing the favourite on the -0.5 line and, in-play, dutching the draw to protect a fragile lead. The Asian handicap guide explains how those lines behave in live matches.
  • "Team to score or over" bundles — dutching two related markets such as "Team A to score" and "Over 2.5 goals" when you expect a high-scoring match but cannot separate the two.
  • In-play hedging — after backing a team that has gone a goal up, dutching the draw and the opposition as a partial insurance policy. This overlaps with hedge betting but with equal-profit sizing.
  • Multiple-bookmaker prices — dutching across two bookmakers (or an exchange vs a bookmaker) to guarantee a margin when their odds disagree badly.

The Four Mistakes That Destroy Dutching Value

Dutching has a deserved reputation for quietly leaking money. The reasons are consistent:

  • Diluting a strong view. If you genuinely believe a favourite wins 70% of the time, dutching the draw at 25% merely converts a profitable single into a break-even pair. The more selections you add, the more the combined margin eats your edge.
  • Dutching for its own sake. Covering three or four outcomes in a match you know nothing about is not risk management — it is a way of turning one losing bet into several smaller losing bets.
  • Ignoring the bookmaker margin. A single book's odds on home/draw/away usually add up to 104–107%. Dutch two of those three outcomes and the margin is working against you twice. The technique only pays when the combined probability you assign is materially below the combined implied probability of the prices.
  • Forgetting it is still a gamble. Dutching reduces variance but never removes risk. The "unlikely" outcome — the away win, the 0-0, the red card — still lands far more often than bettors assume.

Dutching vs Other Risk Tools

Dutching is often confused with three neighbouring techniques, and it pays to know the difference:

  • Double Chance (1X, X2, 12) is a single market that pays on two of the three match outcomes. It is effectively a bookmaker-packaged dutch of two outcomes, but the odds are already adjusted to the market margin — so it is rarely the best price available. Building your own dutch lets you split stakes by your probabilities rather than the bookmaker's.
  • Hedging means placing a later bet to reduce the risk of an earlier one, often locking in a profit or limiting a loss. Dutching is usually executed in one go. See our hedge betting guide for the difference in practice.
  • Laying on an exchange lets you sell an outcome — you profit when it loses. A lay on the away team plus a back on the draw is mathematically a dutch, but via the exchange route you pay commission rather than margin. Our lay betting guide covers that route.

Staking and Tracking Dutch Bets

Because a dutch is technically two or three separate wagers, it must be tracked as one position. Record the combined stake, the total potential return, and the outcome as a single row in your betting journal — otherwise your record keeping will show phantom "losses" on the losing leg of winning dutches and you will misread your true edge.

On staking, most bettors size a dutch against their normal single-bet unit. The flat vs variable staking debate applies unchanged: the combined stake is what matters, not the individual legs.

Bottom line: Dutching is a disciplined way to express "this outcome can't happen" while keeping your win rate high. Use it sparingly, only where your two selections are genuinely under-priced, and never as a substitute for a real betting edge.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is dutching in football betting?

Dutching means splitting a single stake across two or more selections in the same event so that if any of them wins, the profit is the same. A common football example is dutching the home win and the draw when you believe the away side has almost no chance — you cover both outcomes that you think contain the value.

How do I split my stake when dutching?

You stake each selection in proportion to its odds so the payout is identical whichever selection lands. The formula divides the total stake so that each outcome returns the same amount, leaving the difference between that return and the total stake as the guaranteed profit if any selected outcome wins.

When does dutching actually create value?

Dutching creates value when you believe two or more outcomes are collectively under-priced, such as home-win and draw when the draw price is too high. It also reduces variance and risk by covering multiple plausible outcomes. When the selections are genuinely mispriced in your favour, the combined edge beats backing a single outcome.

What mistakes destroy dutching value?

The four big mistakes are dutching outcomes that are not actually mispriced, over-covering so the combined stake gets spread too thin, ignoring the bookmaker margin on each leg, and failing to track dutch bets properly in your records. Dutching when there is no collective edge simply multiplies the margin across more legs.

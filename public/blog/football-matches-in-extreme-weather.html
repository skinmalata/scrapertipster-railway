Football Matches Played in Extreme Weather

Football is a winter sport played in the open, which means it has always been at the mercy of the elements. These are the matches that were played — or cancelled — in the most extreme weather imaginable.

Footballers play in rain, wind, sleet and frost as a matter of routine. Every winter brings a 'snow match' and every summer a heatwave. But occasionally the weather stops being a backdrop and becomes the story — the reason the game was abandoned, postponed or played in conditions that bordered on the absurd.

The Big Freeze of 1963

The most famous weather event in English football history is the winter of 1963, the coldest in the country for over two centuries. From late December to early March, snow and ice gripped Britain, and football shut down almost entirely:

  • FA Cup ties were postponed again and again, with some clubs waiting more than a month to play.
  • Bolton Wanderers went from December to February without playing a single competitive game.
  • The FA Cup final was pushed back to the end of May — the latest ever — and the whole season stretched four weeks beyond its normal finish.

The freeze had one lasting consequence: bookmakers had taken bets on football fixtures that were not being played, so the Pools Panel was created — a group of experts who 'decided' results of postponed matches so the pools could continue. The panel survived for decades.

The 2022 World Cup: Moving Football to Avoid the Heat

The ultimate example of football bending to extreme weather is the 2022 World Cup in Qatar. Qatar's summer temperatures regularly exceed 40C, which is dangerous for players and spectators, so FIFA made the unprecedented decision to move the tournament to November and December — the first World Cup ever played outside the traditional May-to-July window. Every stadium was air-conditioned, and matches kicked off in cooler evening temperatures. It was the largest weather-driven change in the history of international football.

The Snow Matches

Snow produces football's most beautiful photographs and some of its most memorable matches. A pitch covered in snow changes everything: passing becomes impossible, the ball stops dead, and games often descend into chaotic, mud-soaked battles decided by a single bounce.

Famous snow matches include the 'blizzard games' played across the United States and northern Europe, where officials have insisted the game continue despite visibility dropping to a few metres. In some of these matches, the pitch markings disappeared entirely and the lines had to be re-painted in yellow or the players simply played without them.

Monsoon and Torrential Rain

In tropical leagues, the monsoon season turns pitches into swimming pools, and a handful of matches have become legendary for being played in conditions closer to a water park than a football ground:

  • South Asian and South-East Asian leagues routinely reschedule around monsoon seasons, but flood matches still slip through.
  • In one famous European case, a Champions League game was played in such torrential rain that the pitch was covered in standing water and the ball stopped moving entirely mid-pass.

Extreme Heat at Sea Level

Heat affects football in ways snow cannot: it is a genuine health risk. FIFA introduced cooling breaks for tournaments played in hot conditions — a two-to-three minute break after the 30th minute of each half when temperatures become extreme, with the lost time added on as stoppage time. Those breaks, and the unusually long stoppage-time figures they create, have become a regular feature of summer tournaments.

The rule of thumb: Football is designed to be played in almost any weather, but only up to a point. Referees have the power to suspend matches for dangerous conditions — and across history, the extreme has rarely won the argument.

After Drogba: How Ivory Coast Rebuilt Their Identity Around a Generation Europe Almost Forgot

On April 18, 2015, the Elephants of Ivory Coast gathered in the centre of the Stade de Paoua in Bata, Equatorial Guinea, and lifted the Africa Cup of Nations trophy. They had beaten Ghana 9-8 on penalties in a shootout that lasted 20 kicks. It was their second consecutive AFCON title. It was also the last great triumph of the golden generation. By the time the celebrations ended, the sun was already setting on one of the most remarkable eras in African football history. The Hangover

Ivory Coast's post-Drogba era began with denial. They had Yaya TourÃ© for another two years. They had Gervinho, Serge Aurier, and Eric Bailly. They had enough talent to stay competitive, or so the thinking went. They qualified for the 2017 AFCON and finished third. Then they went to the 2019 AFCON and reached the quarter-finals. The decline was gradual, but the warning signs were everywhere.

The biggest alarm bell was 2018. Ivory Coast failed to qualify for the World Cup for the first time since 2006. They finished second in their qualifying group behind Morocco, a team they had beaten in the 2017 AFCON semi-finals. The 2-0 defeat in Casablanca that sealed their fate was a humiliation. The golden generation had carried them through two World Cups (2006, 2014) with competitive performances. Without them, they could not even get out of a group that included Gabon and Mali.

"We knew we had to rebuild from the ground up," said Patrice Beaumelle, who coached Ivory Coast from 2020 to 2022. "The players who had carried the team for so long were gone. We had to find a new identity. And we had to look in places we had not looked before."

The Diaspora Discovery

Ivory Coast has one of the largest African diaspora populations in Europe, with an estimated 800,000 Ivorians living in France alone. Many of their children grew up in French football academies, played for French youth national teams, and dreamed of playing for Les Bleus. But the French national team is the deepest talent pool in world football. Most of them never got the call.

Ivory Coast's football federation began a systematic campaign to recruit these players. It was not a new idea — Drogba himself was born in Abidjan but moved to France at age five. But the post-2015 generation took it to another level. The federation identified dual-nationality players who had fallen through the cracks of the French system and offered them a clear path to playing for the Elephants. The pitch was simple: you will start for us, you will play in AFCON finals, and you will play in the World Cup.

The strategy worked because French academies had, in many cases, already done the development work. They were already Champions League-calibre players. Europe just did not want them for its national team.

By 2022, when Ivory Coast qualified for the World Cup, more than half of their squad had either been born in France or spent their formative years there. The diaspora had become the identity.

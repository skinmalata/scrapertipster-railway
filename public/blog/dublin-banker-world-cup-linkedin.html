The Dublin Banker Who Messaged His Way to the World Cup

Published: June 15, 2026 | 12 min read

Tip: Looking for in-play football predictions? Click here.

If you had told Roberto Lopes, sitting at his teller station in the EBS branch in Blanchardstown Shopping Centre in 2016, that he would be marking Lamine Yamal at a World Cup ten years later, he would have asked you how much you wanted to borrow.

And yet here he is. On June 15, 2026, Roberto Carlos"Pico" Lopes — former mortgage advisor, former part-time Bohemians defender, proud Dubliner — will walk out at SoFi Stadium in Los Angeles to face Spain at the 2026 FIFA World Cup. He will do so wearing the blue of Cape Verde, a nation of 500,000 people scattered across ten Atlantic islands, 3,000 miles from the Dublin suburb where he grew up. And he will do so because of a LinkedIn message he almost deleted.

"I thought it was a spam message and I took no notice of it." — Roberto Lopes, on the LinkedIn message that changed his life

The Tellers and the Dreamers

In 2016, Lopes was 23 years old and living every League of Ireland player's reality. He played centre-back for Bohemians, one of the oldest and most storied clubs in Irish football, but Bohs, like almost every club in the league at the time, could not offer full-time professional contracts. So Lopes did what Irish footballers have done for decades: he got a day job.

He worked as a cash teller at EBS in Blanchardstown, processing mortgage payments while studying for his mortgage advisor qualification. His routine was a grind: finish a shift at the bank, rush to training, get home late, wake up and do it all again."Bohs were really struggling at the time, and the message coming through was that you can't be a professional footballer in Ireland, you are going to have to get a job," Lopes later recalled. "A few years pass and you get to 23, and you start looking at alternatives."

He hated the desk job."I was not enjoying the work," he said plainly. But football in Ireland in the mid-2010s offered no alternatives. The League of Ireland was producing talent — Seamus Coleman, James McClean, Wes Hoolahan had all come through its ranks — but the structure to keep players full-time simply did not exist for most.

Then came the coffee that changed everything.

The Coffee Meeting

Glenn Cronin, a former Bohemians teammate who had joined Shamrock Rovers' coaching staff, called Lopes. They met for coffee. Cronin told him that Stephen Bradley, Rovers' newly appointed manager, wanted to meet him. Lopes was sceptical. Moving from Bohemians to Shamrock Rovers is not a simple transfer in Dublin — it is crossing the city's fiercest football divide.

But Bradley sold him a vision. Rovers were offering something almost unheard of in the League of Ireland at the time: a full-time professional contract with multi-year security."It was probably groundbreaking at the time for the League of Ireland, where they were giving out that sort of security where you could go and be a footballer for two years and try and make something of yourself," Lopes said.

He signed. He left the bank. He never went back.

The gamble paid off spectacularly. Since joining Shamrock Rovers in 2017, Lopes has won five League of Ireland Premier Division titles, an FAI Cup, and competed in three European group stage campaigns. He has been named in the PFA Ireland Team of the Year multiple times. He became, quietly, one of the most decorated defenders in League of Ireland history.

But international football was a door he assumed had closed. He had earned a single cap for the Republic of Ireland at Under-19 level in 2011, playing alongside Jeff Hendrick. The senior team, stacked with Premier League defenders, was a distant dream."I probably wasn't good enough to be near the team," he admitted.

In 2018, he updated his LinkedIn profile — an account he had created years earlier for a college module and barely touched — and listed his football career alongside his finance background. He connected with a name that popped up: Rui Aguas, the manager of the Cape Verde national team. He thought nothing of it.

Then the message arrived.

The Message

It was written in Portuguese. Lopes does not speak Portuguese. He glanced at it, assumed it was spam or a prank, and ignored it."I grew up in an era of prank phone calls and prank messages so I was always a bit sceptical," he said. He went back to training. Nine months passed.

Then a second message arrived: "Hi Roberto, have you had a chance to consider what I said to you?"

This time, Lopes copied the original message into Google Translate. The translation read: "We're looking at getting new players into the Cape Verde squad. Would you be interested in declaring for Cape Verde?"

"I was absolutely buzzing with that," Lopes said."I was like, 'Yep, 100% I'd love to be a part of the squad.'" He responded immediately, apologising profusely for the nine-month silence.

Aguas, to his credit, did not hold the delay against him. Cape Verde's recruitment strategy under Aguas and his successor Bubista was built on tapping into the Cape Verdean diaspora — the estimated 700,000 people of Cape Verdean descent living abroad, primarily in the Netherlands, Portugal, France, and the United States. Lopes, with his Cape Verdean father Carlos who had left the islands at 16 to work as a chef on international ships before settling in Dublin, was exactly the profile they were targeting.

The LinkedIn Generation

Cape Verde's recruitment strategy under coach Bubista deliberately targeted diaspora players. Six of their 2026 World Cup squad have Dutch roots. Others were identified through Portugal's youth system, French academies, and — in Lopes' case — a professional networking site. The federation hired US-based agent and recruitment specialist Tony Araujo to systematically identify eligible players across the globe.

Only Iceland, with a population of 360,000, has qualified for a World Cup with a smaller domestic base.

From LinkedIn to the World Cup

Lopes made his Cape Verde debut in a friendly against Togo in 2019. He quickly became a fixture in the Blue Sharks' defence, starting nine of Cape Verde's ten World Cup qualifying matches and playing every minute of their run to the Africa Cup of Nations quarter-finals in 2024.

On October 13, 2025, Cape Verde beat Eswatini 3-0 in Praia to qualify for the World Cup for the first time in their history. Lopes was on the pitch. His father Carlos was in the stands. After the final whistle, he rushed to the airport — his wife Leah was expecting their first child back in Dublin. He made it home in time for the birth of his son, Diego.

He became, in that moment, the first League of Ireland player ever to qualify for a World Cup. The Republic of Ireland, meanwhile, had been knocked out in the play-offs by the Czech Republic — meaning Lopes is the only Irish-based player heading to the tournament.

The World Cup

Cape Verde have been drawn in Group H alongside Spain, Uruguay, and Saudi Arabia. They are rank outsiders to progress, but that is precisely the position they have occupied at every tournament they have entered."We are not here by chance, we are here on merit, we have earned our place," Lopes said.

His own journey from a cash teller in Blanchardstown to marking Spain's attackers on the world stage is improbable by any measure. The Crumlin community gave him a surprise send-off in May 2026, lining the streets with Cape Verde flags. His father Carlos, who left Cape Verde at 16 not knowing if he would ever return, watched his son pull on the blue shirt of his homeland."Being able to represent my family playing for the national team and being able to put our family name out there at one of the biggest sporting events in the world fills me with great pride," Lopes said.

He still plays for Shamrock Rovers. He still lives in Dublin. He still checks his LinkedIn messages — these days, a little more carefully.

"From when I was a young child, and I imagine every aspiring footballer when they were young, they wanted to play at the highest level possible and, for me, it doesn't go any further than the World Cup." — Roberto Lopes

What It Means

Roberto Lopes' story is not just a human-interest curiosity. It is a case study in how football's talent landscape is shifting. The traditional pathway — academy to professional contract to national team — is being supplemented by diaspora networks, data-driven scouting, and, yes, LinkedIn. Cape Verde's qualification for the 2026 World Cup is the most striking example yet of a small nation leveraging its global diaspora to compete with the football establishment.

Six of their squad have Dutch roots. Their captain trained in Portugal's youth system. Their centre-back was discovered on a professional networking site by a manager who sent a message in Portuguese and waited nine months for a reply.

And now they are at the World Cup. Spain await. And a former Dublin bank teller who nearly deleted a LinkedIn message is about to write the most improbable chapter in Cape Verdean football history.

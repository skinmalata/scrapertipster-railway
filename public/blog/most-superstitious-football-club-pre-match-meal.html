Football's Most Superstitious Club: The Same Pre-Match Meal Since 1963

Published: July 4, 2026 | 7 min read

Tip: Looking for today's football predictions? Click here.

Football is a game of inches, split-second decisions, and margins so fine that managers and players will grasp at anything for an edge. Science gives them training regimens, analytics, and sports psychology. But superstition gives them something science cannot: control over the uncontrollable. And nowhere is this more visible than in what footballers eat before a match.

Across the world, clubs and players have clung to the same pre-match meals for decades, convinced that changing the menu would break a winning spell. Some of these traditions have lasted so long that nobody remembers how they started. They just know you do not mess with them.

The Spaghetti Bolognese That Won Trophies

In 1975, West Ham United won the FA Cup. The players celebrated, the fans rejoiced, and the club's chef served spaghetti bolognese the day before the final. The team won. So the chef served it again before the next match. They won again. Before long, spaghetti bolognese became a fixed part of West Ham's matchday routine.

What started as an ordinary meal turned into a decades-long tradition. West Ham served spaghetti bolognese to players the day before every single match, from the 1975 FA Cup win straight through to the modern era. Players came and went. Managers were hired and fired. But the spaghetti bolognese never left the menu. The players themselves credited the meal for their success, and the club saw no reason to argue with results. When something works, you stick with it.

Dalglish's Never-Changing Menu

Kenny Dalglish, Liverpool's legendary forward and later manager, was so superstitious about his pre-match meal that he ate the exact same dinner every Friday night throughout his playing career. The menu never varied: steak pie with potatoes and peas, tomato soup, and apple pie with custard for dessert.

His wife Marina once asked if he would like something different. Dalglish's response has become part of football folklore: "Never change a winning menu." He refused to deviate. Every Friday, without fail, Dalglish sat down to the same plate. The ritual gave him certainty in an uncertain profession. When you know what you are eating, you can focus on what matters: winning the match.

Racing Club's Choripan Miracle

Perhaps the most extreme example of club-wide superstition comes from Argentina. During Racing Club de Avellaneda's title-winning 2001 campaign, manager Reinaldo "Mostaza" Merlo started each matchday with a choripan — a simple Argentine sausage sandwich — from the same street vendor near the stadium. The team kept winning. So Merlo kept eating.

Then came the match against Boca Juniors. For reasons nobody can explain, Merlo did not have his choripan that day. Racing lost. The message was clear: the choripan was not optional. From that moment on, the entire squad adopted dozens of "cábalas" (superstitions). Players wore the same socks, sat in the same seats on the bus, entered the pitch with the same foot first. The club's run continued, and the superstitions only multiplied. It is a perfect snapshot of how quickly a single good-luck charm can snowball into an entire belief system.

More Club Rituals

West Ham, Dalglish, and Racing Club are far from alone. Liverpool had an unspoken rule: players had to sit in the same seats on the team bus every matchday and could only order from three meal options — fish and chips, or spaghetti. Pepe Reina, Liverpool's goalkeeper, ate cheese and ham toasties with a glass of wine the night before every game, stopped at the same gas station on matchdays, and parked in spot #39 at the training ground. If spot #39 was taken, he waited.

Paul Scholes always ate beans on toast before a match, accompanied by two Turkish Delight chocolates. He bought a pack of three and left one behind, because the ritual demanded it. Sir Alex Ferguson chewed the same flavour of gum during every Manchester United match. If United were losing, he would spit out the piece and start a new one — as if the gum itself had let him down.

Why Footballers Are So Superstitious

Elite sport is defined by randomness. A deflection, a referee's decision, a gust of wind — any of these can decide a match. Superstition gives players the illusion of control. Eating the same meal, following the same routine, wearing the same boots: these rituals reduce anxiety and create a psychological anchor. When a player steps onto the pitch knowing every variable they can control is locked in, they perform better. Neuroscience backs this up. Familiar rituals lower cortisol levels and improve focus under pressure.

There is also a powerful social element. When a whole squad adopts the same superstitions, it reinforces team identity. West Ham's spaghetti bolognese was not just a meal — it was a shared belief that the club had found a winning formula.

Betting Relevance

For bettors, understanding club superstitions and rituals can offer a genuine edge. If a club has a long-standing pre-match tradition that gets disrupted — a change of hotel, a different menu, a travel delay — it can affect team morale and performance. Likewise, knowing which players are deeply ritualistic helps when assessing individual player performance markets.

If Liverpool's team bus seating order is thrown off by a new signing, or if West Ham's chef calls in sick on spaghetti day, those are small signals that a bookmaker's algorithm will never capture. The best bettors factor in the human element. Superstition is part of football's psychology, and psychology is part of the odds.

The Superstition Edge: Before placing a bet, check if a team's routine has been disrupted. A change in pre-match meal, travel plans, or even the manager's usual matchday habits can be a subtle but meaningful signal.
